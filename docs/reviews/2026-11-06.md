## 1. Strongest new finding added to the register

### Brief mindfulness inductions: small acute executive-function effect

**Shi, K., Wimmer, L., Calia, C., et al. (2026).** *Acute Effects of Brief Mindfulness Inductions on Executive Function Performance: A Systematic Review and Three-Level Meta-Analysis*. Neuropsychology Review. Published 6 November 2026. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11065-026-09714-9

The full open-access article was reviewed. This preregistered three-level meta-analysis included 66 studies, 187 effect sizes and 5,014 participants across mindfulness and control groups. It examined single-session inductions and immediate task outcomes. The pooled effect on overall executive function was very small (g = 0.09, 95% CI [0.03, 0.16]). The inhibitory-control effect was small and significant; working-memory and cognitive-flexibility subgroup estimates did not reach significance. The authors said the practical relevance was limited.

The evidence base also had important weaknesses: 23 studies included a mindfulness-state manipulation check (19 with usable data); four studies were rated high risk of bias; 84.85% had some risk-of-bias concerns. Most samples were non-clinical, but the review did not establish a reliable adolescent-specific effect. Age moderation was underpowered.

**Product decision:** Do not make a brief mindfulness exercise a core cognitive-training module or describe it as a meaningful intelligence/EF boost. It may be an optional, non-scored pause or reflection activity. Acute state effects must remain distinct from durable training effects. This guardrail was added to the blueprint and source 14 was added to the evidence register.

## 2. Related evidence considered, not treated as post-review publications

These studies were found during the broader search but were published before the existing 2026-11-06 register marker. They are noted for context and are not described as newly published since that marker.

- **Exercise and adolescent executive function:** Li, D. et al. (2026), *Effects of exercise interventions on executive function in adolescents*, Frontiers in Psychiatry, published 27 October 2026. https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2026.1968917. Full text reviewed. Twenty-two RCTs (1,599 adolescents aged 10–19) produced a pooled executive-function estimate of g = 0.55, but heterogeneity was very high (I² = 84.92%). GRADE certainty was low for overall EF, inhibition and working memory, and moderate for cognitive flexibility. The estimate is not a prediction of benefit for every person or exercise programme; it supports cautious health education, not a specific app prescription.
- **Classroom active breaks:** Li, C. et al. (2026), *Effects of active breaks on attention and executive function in children and adolescents*, European Journal of Pediatrics, published 24 August 2026. https://doi.org/10.1007/s00431-026-07341-3. The available publisher page and PubMed record report 25 RCTs with pooled estimates favoring attention and inhibition, plus working memory and cognitive flexibility. Considerable heterogeneity and uncertain exploratory subgroup results mean 5–15 minutes is not an established optimum. Full text was paywalled; findings were checked against the publisher preview and PubMed record, not a complete full-text analysis.
- **Computerized working-memory training:** Li, G., Liu, Y., & Chen, A. (2026), *Meta-analysis of computerised working memory training: behavioural gains, training parameters, transfer mechanisms, and neural correlates*, npj Digital Medicine, published 12 March 2026. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41746-026-02478-9. The full PMC record was reviewed. It synthesized 45 neuroimaging studies (1,492 participants; mean age 33.71 years, range 11.9–69.6; 67.27% cognitively healthy) and reported a pooled cognitive-task effect g = 0.503. However, 43.67% of studies measured trained-task outcomes and the pooled estimate did not establish far transfer; the age mix and outcome mix limit inference for adolescents and general everyday cognition.
- **Computerized cognitive training in older adults with mild cognitive impairment:** Luo, H. et al. (2026), *Cognitive transfer and durability of computerised cognitive training in older adults with mild cognitive impairment*, Frontiers in Aging Neuroscience, published 15 October 2026. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnagi.2026.1952783. The PMC full-text record was reviewed. Eight independent RCTs (722 participants) were included; none was at overall low risk of bias. Immediate near/far transfer was imprecise and very-low certainty; sustained near/far estimates were close to zero but based on few trials. This is a clinically distinct older-adult population and cannot be directly generalized to healthy adolescents; it reinforces the need to test transfer and durability separately.

## 3. Creativity, social cognition, emotional skills and other domains

- A very recent early-adolescent creativity meta-analysis (Chamansara et al., 2026; published 1 November 2026) reports promising divergent-thinking results, but the quantitative synthesis for that outcome included only nine study groups and showed substantial heterogeneity. Scientific and mathematical creativity estimates were imprecise or not statistically significant in the inclusive analyses. The article is available at https://doi.org/10.61838/3pwz6p30. It was not used to upgrade NeuroForge's creativity evidence label because of the small outcome-specific evidence base and the need for further independent quality appraisal.
- A separate meta-analysis of constructivist teaching approaches reports a positive creative-thinking estimate (g = 0.68; 55 studies, 7,490 students), DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2026.100856. The publisher's abstract/preview was accessible, but full text was not; it is treated as a lead, not as a fully verified basis for changing NeuroForge's claims. Its classroom pedagogy findings should not be equated with an app-based brain-training effect.
- The search did not identify a newly published, fully verified source within this same-day window that warrants changing the CHC-informed ability map, social-cognition framework, or emotional-intelligence measurement rules. This is not proof that no such research exists; it reflects the scope of this targeted scan.

## 4. Limitations of this review

This was a targeted evidence scan, not a preregistered systematic review. The prior register marker and current date are both 2026-11-06, so the review period is effectively same-day. Some publisher pages exposed only abstracts or previews; those items are explicitly identified. Search indexing dates are not reliable substitutes for publication dates. No source was treated as proof that NeuroForge itself works.

## 5. NeuroForge decisions

1. Keep the CHC-informed cognitive map and evidence labels; no framework rewrite is justified by this scan.
2. Add the brief-mindfulness guardrail: optional non-scored pause only, no substantial EF/intelligence claim.
3. Retain the priority of measurement integrity, independent baseline assessment, varied tasks, and separate near/far transfer testing.
4. Keep cognitive-health guidance optional and age-appropriate. Exercise and classroom-break findings are promising but heterogeneous; do not prescribe an optimal duration or claim that NeuroForge's game sessions cause the same effects.
5. Do not change application code based only on these papers.
